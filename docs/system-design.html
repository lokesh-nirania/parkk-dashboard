§ 01 — Problem & scope

What this system is actually for

The company runs several ship repair projects at once, across countries, with workers sourced from other countries again. The planning that makes a project possible lives in spreadsheets, WhatsApp threads and people's heads.

The stated pain is precise, and worth quoting back: tomorrow is a start date, and there is no single interface to check whether every worker has immigration and accommodation settled, whether they have reached site, whether logistics are sorted, whether equipment has shipped and arrived, and what is still pending.

That sentence is the product. Everything else in this document exists to make that one screen answerable in a single database query. Two consequences follow, and they should shape every later decision:

  • This is a tracking system, not a doing system. Visas are filed elsewhere. Quotes are built in Excel. Flights are booked on airline sites. Paint is bought from a supplier. The dashboard records state and evidence, and its value is aggregation — not replacing any of those workflows.
  • Status must be structured enough to aggregate. A free-text note saying "visa in progress" is worth nothing to a portfolio board. The discipline of this design is deciding exactly which things get typed status and which do not.

In scope for v1

  • Admin role only — a small number of trusted users (the Abhi / Varsha level from the current tracker) doing all data entry.
  • Quotation pipeline through project delivery through invoicing status.
  • Both project types: hull jobs and third-party supervision.
  • Multi-country, multi-currency as data, not as tax or accounting logic.

Explicit non-goals

  • No integration with the Parkk Marine website or portal. Standalone system, confirmed.
  • Not a quoting or pricing engine — the estimate is authored outside and attached here.
  • Not an accounting system — invoices are tracked as sent / paid, nothing more.
  • Not an HRIS or payroll system, and not a warehouse stock system of record (see §8 for the boundary).
  • No client-facing portal, no PM mobile app, no worker self-service — all deliberately deferred, but the data model in §5 is shaped so they can be added without a rewrite.
Design posture

Build for one honest board and many boring forms. The forms are unglamorous data entry; the board is the entire return on that entry. Any modelling choice that makes the board weaker to make a form prettier is the wrong choice.

§ 02 — The central design call

Typed where you aggregate, generic where you adapt

You arrived at the generic-task idea mid-sentence and then immediately felt its flaw. That instinct was right, and it deserves to be the architectural spine rather than a passing doubt.

A fully generic task tree ("task has subtasks, subtasks have progress") is seductive because it models anything. But it cannot answer "have all required consumables reached site?" — because that question is not about completion, it is about coverage: 40 of 60 drums delivered is neither done nor not-done. Nor can it answer "is anyone's insurance going to lapse mid-project?", which is about validity windows, not status. Generic tasks flatten exactly the structure the dashboard exists to surface.

The opposite failure is equally real: hard-coding every requirement type means a new client demanding drug tests, or a new country demanding a police clearance, becomes a schema migration.

So: three layers, with a sharp test for which layer a thing belongs in.

The test

Would an admin ever want to filter, count, or traffic-light this across projects? If yes, it is a typed trackable with typed fields. If no, it is a checklist item or a free task. "Passport scan collected" passes the test. "Call the yard about the crane slot" does not.

Layer 1 — Trackables

Modelled records for the things that recur on every project and drive readiness: assignments, immigration cases, travel legs, accommodation, yard passes, insurance policies, equipment allocations, consumable lines, shipments, reporting obligations, invoices. Each has real fields — a visa case has a country and a stage, a shipment has an ETA.

Layer 2 — The tracking contract

This is the load-bearing idea. Every trackable, whatever its type, exposes the same small set of fields (§6). Because a visa case and a paint delivery both carry status, due_date, responsible_party and valid_from/valid_to, entirely different things roll up onto one board without special-casing. The contract is what makes heterogeneity survivable.

Layer 3 — Tasks & checklists

A generic, polymorphic task attachable to any entity, with subtasks, assignee and due date — for the long tail your notes describe as "tasks (jobs)". Plus project-level checklist templates for the per-client oddities. These roll up as a single extra dimension, so they are visible without polluting the typed ones.

The pattern to keep in mind: Layer 1 says what exists, Layer 2 says how it is going, Layer 3 catches what we did not anticipate. When a Layer 3 checklist item shows up on 80% of projects, promote it to Layer 1.

Quotation state machine

Quotes are versioned and immutable. A revision creates a new version rather than editing the old one, so "what did we actually send in March?" always has an answer, and win/loss analysis has something to analyse. One version is flagged current.

DRAFT→ SENT→ UNDER_CLIENT_REVIEW→ ACCEPTED→ CONVERTED REVISION_REQUESTED↺ new version →DRAFT | DECLINED→CLOSED_LOST | EXPIRED|WITHDRAWN

On ACCEPTED → CONVERTED, the system creates the project and copies the estimated headcount into manpower requirement rows as a starting plan. This is the single highest-value automation in v1: it means planning begins pre-populated instead of from a blank page, and it makes the estimate-versus-actual comparison free later.

Project state machine

Your own status list, formalised. Note that PLANNING and IN_PROGRESS overlap in reality — see the caveat below.

QUOTING→ CONFIRMED→ PLANNING→ MOBILIZING→ IN_PROGRESS→ DEMOBILIZING→ INVOICING→ CLOSED any state →CANCELLED ·ON_HOLD

I have split MOBILIZING out of planning deliberately: planning is deciding what is needed, mobilizing is waiting for the world to deliver it — visas, flights, shipments. They have completely different management responses, and the pain you described lives entirely in the second one.

Important — planning never closes

Your note that scope creep means "rearrange travels and more people, take confirmations, change travel plans" breaks the naive phase model. A variation raised in week three re-opens manpower, immigration and travel for new people while the project is IN_PROGRESS.

So: phases describe the project's centre of gravity, not a gate. Readiness dimensions stay live and computable for the whole project lifetime, and the board must be able to show an in-progress project with a red immigration light. Do not build "planning complete" as an irreversible transition.

§ 06 — Readiness engine

The contract that makes one board possible

A visa case, a hotel booking and sixty drums of primer have nothing in common as data. They have everything in common as things that must be true before Tuesday. This section defines the shared shape that lets them share a screen.

The tracking contract

Every trackable carries these fields, whatever else it carries. This is the whole trick.

FieldTypeWhy it earns its place
statusenumThe traffic light. Vocabulary below — shared across every type, no exceptions.
responsible_partyenumUS · CLIENT · AGENCY · VENDOR · AUTHORITY · WORKER. Directly from "some clients book the flights themselves". Enables the single most useful filter in the system: show me everything the client owes us.
owner_user_idfkThe Abhi / Varsha / PM column from the existing tracker. Gives "my open items" and "who is drowning".
due_datedateWhen it must be done by — usually derived from a lead time before the date it enables.

qty_required · qty_fulfillednumericFor coverage-typed items. 40 of 60 drums is a real state that status alone cannot express.
blocked_reasontextMandatory when status is BLOCKED. Turns a red light into an action.
evidence_document_idfkProof. Prevents the failure mode where someone ticks a box optimistically.

Why awaiting_external is not just in_progress

In this business, a large share of every project is spent waiting on somebody else. Collapsing "we have not started the visa" and "we filed the visa five weeks ago and the consulate is silent" into one amber light destroys the board's usefulness — the first needs someone to act, the second needs someone to chase. Two different states, two different Monday mornings.

Three kinds of dimension

Not every readiness question has the same arithmetic. Conflating them is the mistake the generic-task model makes.

KindQuestion it answersRuleUsed by
ChecklistIs every required item done?All children DONE or N/AImmigration, insurance, yard access, PM assignment
CoverageDo we have enough?Σ fulfilled ≥ Σ requiredManpower, consumables, equipment
WindowIs it valid when we need it?valid_from ≤ need_from AND valid_to ≥ need_toVisas, work permits, yard passes, insurance, equipment certs

The window rule is the direct answer to two of your notes — "insurance for travel period" and "shipyard access: people who have access to this much time". A yard pass valid to 20 March on a project running to 4 April is not green, even though someone marked it done. The system should compute that gap and surface it, because nobody will notice it by eye across forty workers.

Rollup, worked

Trackables reduce to dimensions; dimensions reduce to a project light. Worst-state-wins, with a completion percentage alongside so a project that is 90% ready and one that is 20% ready do not look identical.

Derived, never stored by hand

  • at_risk — not DONE and due_date falls inside the item's lead time. A visa with a 21-day lead time goes amber 21 days out, not the day it is late.
  • window_gap — validity fails to cover the required window (above).
  • expired — valid_to is in the past.
  • coverage_shortfall — qty_required − qty_fulfilled.
  • days_to_start — the sort key for the whole portfolio board.

Lead times belong to the requirement type per corridor, not to the record — "Indian passport into the US" has a different visa lead time than "Mexican passport into the US". Seed them with the company's own numbers (question 34 in §15) and let admins override per project.

§ 07 — Mobilization model

Getting people to the yard

One assignment, several clearances, each owned by a possibly different party. This is where the dashboard earns its keep and where the modelling is subtlest.

Slots before names

Planning starts before people are chosen — you said as much: "3 plumbers", not "Ravi, Suresh, Mateo". So ManpowerRequirement declares a count and Assignment rows are created as unfilled slots against it. A slot can already carry a due date and an owner; when a worker is named, the mobilization items attach to the same slot and nothing is lost.

This gives the manpower dimension its coverage arithmetic — 9 of 12 slots filled — which is a distinct and important failure mode from 12 filled, 3 without visas. The board should never conflate them.

Which clearances apply

Requirements vary by nationality, destination, client and yard. The eventual answer is a rule matrix:

(worker_nationality, destination_country, client, shipyard, role) → required MobilizationItem kinds

For v1 that is over-engineering. Instead: a mobilization template per project, defaulted from the destination country and the yard's stored requirements, then edited by the admin. Every generated item exists as a record even when unnecessary — marked not_applicable — so the matrix is complete and honest rather than sparse and ambiguous. The rule engine can be introduced later behind the same interface.

The client-books-travel case

You flagged that some clients book flights and accommodation themselves and hand over tickets. That is not an exception to model around — it is exactly what responsible_party = CLIENT is for. The item still exists, still has a due date, still shows red if the tickets have not arrived by T−7. The difference is only who gets chased. Same for yards issuing passes and agencies arranging insurance.

The passport problem

Some clients require the worker list with passport details to issue yard passes. That means this system will hold passport numbers and scans, which changes its risk profile considerably. Design response in §12: identity data lives in a separate restricted table, access is logged, and the client submission is a deliberate export pack action that records who exported what, for which yard, and when — rather than passport fields sitting on a screen anyone can browse.

§ 08 — Logistics & custody

Getting things to the yard, and away again

Two questions, repeatedly: do we have enough, and where is it right now.

Requirement → pack → ship → receive

Your note gives the chain exactly: equipment required → packing consumables from warehouse → arrange shipping. Modelled as requirement lines whose fulfilment is driven by shipments:

REQUESTED→ALLOCATED→PACKED→DISPATCHED→IN_TRANSIT→CUSTOMS→DELIVERED_TO_YARD→RECEIVED_ON_SITE

Delivered and received are separate states on purpose. A consignment sitting at a yard gate that nobody has signed for is a very common and very expensive kind of "arrived". Only the PM's receipt closes the loop.

Consumables are coverage-typed: qty_required → qty_packed → qty_delivered → qty_consumed. Partial deliveries are the norm, so the board reports "consumables 47/60 lines on site" rather than a binary. This is precisely the question — "have all required consumables reached site?" — that motivated rejecting the pure task model.

The warehouse boundary

Scope decision — confirmed

The warehouse is a separate concern. This system will never answer "what is the inventory of warehouse A right now?" — no stock levels, no reorder points, no valuation. It tracks only what a project needs and whether it arrived, referencing item codes that live elsewhere. Resist every future request to grow this into an inventory system; that is a different product with a different owner.

Equipment custody across projects low priority

Equipment picked from a warehouse is assigned to a project, then either moves on to another project or returns to the warehouse. That chain is worth tracking — it means equipment has a life between projects, not just within one — but it is explicitly not a v1 priority. Model the fields now so the history is capturable; build the custody views in a later phase (§13).

  • Equipment.current_location is a first-class field: a warehouse, a project site, in transit, or with a rental vendor.
  • EquipmentAllocation.return_destination is decided at demob: WAREHOUSE, PROJECT:{id}, or VENDOR for rentals.
  • A transfer to another project creates that project's inbound allocation automatically — so the receiving project's equipment dimension turns green from the transfer, not from a warehouse dispatch that never happens.
  • Rented equipment carries rental start/end and vendor, so an overdue return is a cost that shows up as a red light rather than as a surprise invoice.

Combined with certification expiry on the asset, this also answers "is the equipment we are shipping actually certified for the whole job" — the same window rule from §6, applied to things instead of people.

What v1 actually needs is much smaller: for each project, is the required equipment allocated, dispatched and received on site. The custody chain above is the same records read a different way, so building the simple version first costs nothing later.

§ 09 — Execution & variations

Once work starts

The existing tracker lists it plainly: commencement, daily meetings, daily progress report, WhatsApp with client, daily tasks, bottleneck reporting, scope creep, final reports.

Obligations, not tasks

A daily progress report is not a task — it is a recurring duty with a period. Modelling it as tasks means generating hundreds of rows and then having no clean way to ask "which projects missed yesterday?".

So: ReportingObligation defines the duty (kind, frequency, audience), and ObligationLog holds one row per period with done | missed | not_required plus a link to wherever the report actually lives. Cheap to build, and it produces one of the highest-value screens in the system — a compliance grid of projects × days.

The same mechanism covers the supervision project's client reporting, which you described as tracked only as reporting done or not. That is exactly what this is.

Timezone

"Daily" is ambiguous across a portfolio spanning several countries. Each project carries a timezone, and an obligation period is a local date at the project site. Otherwise the board will report false misses every evening in one hemisphere.

WhatsApp

The team lives on WhatsApp with clients, and no dashboard will change that. Do not attempt to ingest or replace it. Track the one fact that matters for visibility — was the client updated today — as an obligation like any other, optionally with a pasted link or a one-line note. Anything more ambitious is a phase-3 conversation about a WhatsApp Business API integration, and should not hold up v1.

Blockers

"Bottleneck reporting" becomes a first-class Blocker: category (manpower · logistics · client · yard · technical · weather · commercial), severity, impact in days, owner, resolution. Two payoffs — a cross-project blocker feed on the portfolio board, and, after a year, an honest answer to what actually delays our projects. Categorise from day one; retrofitting categories onto free text is impossible.

Variations, and the loop back into planning

Scope creep is the most structurally interesting thing in your notes, because it is the one flow that runs backwards through the lifecycle:

variation identified→costed→submitted to client→approved→new manpower requirement→new assignments→re-open travel & immigration→amend existing travel

Consequences for the design, all of which follow from taking that loop seriously:

  • ManpowerRequirement carries an optional variation_id — so "original scope" and "variation scope" stay distinguishable for both readiness and billing.
  • Mobilization items are generated for late-added assignments with compressed lead times, which the at-risk rule will immediately flag red. That is correct and desirable: a worker added three weeks before the end of a project genuinely may not get a visa in time, and the board should say so on day one rather than at the airport.
  • Amending existing travel — your "change travel plans" — needs the original booking preserved. Keep an amendment history on travel items, including change fees, because those are recoverable costs that currently get lost.
  • An approved variation should be able to generate an invoice line. That link is what makes scope creep visible commercially rather than only operationally.

Demobilization

The mirror of mobilization, and routinely forgotten until it is expensive: return flights, hotel checkout, yard pass surrender, equipment return with a destination decision (§8), consumable reconciliation (delivered vs consumed vs returned vs written off), final report, client sign-off. Reuse the same trackable machinery with a demob flag rather than inventing new types.

§ 10 — Commercial

Quote in, invoices out

Deliberately thin. This is a status tracker sitting beside the finance process, not inside it.

Quotation

Covered as a state machine in §4. The content model is intentionally loose: a scope summary, estimated headcount by role, an estimated duration, proposed dates, a value, a validity date, and the actual client-facing file as an attachment. The dashboard displays and tracks; Excel still authors.

Two fields worth insisting on despite being optional-feeling: valid_until, so stale quotes expire themselves rather than lingering as fake pipeline; and win_loss_reason as a short enum, because after thirty quotes it answers "why are we losing" for the price of one dropdown.

Invoicing

Per your note — multiple invoices per project, sent out versus paid. A flat list per project:

DRAFT→RAISED→SENT→ACKNOWLEDGED→PAID ·OVERDUE(derived from due_at)·DISPUTED

Each invoice optionally references a milestone or an approved variation. The project shows invoiced-to-date against value, and the portfolio shows total outstanding and overdue. No ledger, no tax logic, no payment integration — those belong to whatever accounting system exists, and this system should link to it, not duplicate it.

Deliberately absent

Cost tracking against budget is not in v1. It is a natural and valuable extension — every mobilization item already has a cost field, so project cost accumulates for free — but it introduces approval workflows and finance stakeholders that would delay the readiness board. Collect the data now, build the view later. See question 74.

A note on data entry

All of this depends on admins keeping status current, which is the real adoption risk (§16). Two mitigations belong in v1: bulk edit on the mobilization matrix — select twelve workers, set visa to filed — and CSV import for worker lists and consumable lines. If updating twelve visas takes twelve page loads, the spreadsheet wins and the project fails.

§ 12 — Schema & stack

How it is stored and built

One structural decision matters more than all the rest: how the tracking contract is physically stored.

The storage question

OptionUpsideDownside
Table per typeClean typed columns, easy constraintsEvery board becomes a twelve-way UNION; every new requirement kind is a migration and a UI change
One generic tableBoards are one query; new kinds are freeWeak constraints, everything in JSONB, unqueryable detail
Hybrid — recommendedBoards are one query and rich domains keep real columnsTwo places to write on some operations; needs discipline about which fields live where

Recommendation: the hybrid. A single tracked_item table implements the §6 contract for everything, and rich domains keep satellite tables joined one-to-one for the detail that deserves real columns.

Satellite tables for the domains with genuine structure: assignment, shipment, consumable_line, equipment_allocation, obligation_log, invoice. The rule of thumb for which side a field goes on: if a board filters or sorts on it, it is a column on tracked_item; if only a detail drawer displays it, it is JSONB or a satellite.

Rollups

Recompute the project's dimension summary on write, inside the same transaction that changes an item, and store it on a project_readiness row. The portfolio board then reads one small table with no aggregation at all. A nightly job recomputes everything anyway, because date-derived states (at-risk, expired) change without any write occurring — that job, not user activity, is what keeps the board honest over a quiet weekend.

Personal data

Treat this as a real constraint, not a checkbox

The system holds passport numbers, scans, visa documents and medical certificates for foreign nationals across multiple jurisdictions. Minimum design response for v1:

  • worker_identity is a separate table with its own access path — never joined into list views by default.
  • Document files live in private object storage behind short-lived signed URLs. Never a public bucket.
  • Every read of identity data and every export writes an ActivityEvent. When a client asks who sent their yard a passport list, the answer exists.
  • Sharing with a client or yard is an explicit export pack action — choose recipient, choose fields, log it — not a copy-paste from a screen.
  • A retention policy field per document kind, and a job that flags identity documents for deletion N days after project close. Ask the company for N (question 77).

§ 14 — Decisions for you

Calls to make before writing code

These are yours, not the company's — they do not depend on any answer from the client. Each carries my recommendation so none of them blocks you.

Are workers first-class records in v1, or just headcounts?

Recommend first-class. Immigration, travel, insurance and yard passes are all per person — modelling only "3 plumbers" makes the crux screen impossible to build. Unfilled slots (§7) let planning start before names exist, so you get both.

One tracked_item table, or a table per requirement type?

Recommend the hybrid in §12. Pure per-type tables make every board a painful union; pure generic makes the detail views mush.

Rule engine for which clearances apply, or an editable per-project template?

Recommend template now, rules later. The nationality × destination matrix is real but you cannot populate it accurately without months of the company's data. A defaulted, admin-editable list gets you there with a tenth of the work and no wrong answers.

Host documents, or link out to Drive?

Recommend links first, uploads for identity documents only. They already live in Drive or email; forcing a re-upload of everything is adoption friction. Passports and visas are the exception — those need the controlled path in §12.

Rollups computed on read or on write?

Recommend on write into a summary table, plus a nightly recompute for date-derived states. Portfolio-wide aggregation on read will be fine at 20 projects and unpleasant at 200, and the nightly job is needed regardless.

How much timeline? Full Gantt with dependencies?

Recommend no. Milestones plus phase bars. A dependency-aware Gantt is a product in itself, and the pain being solved is readiness, not scheduling. Revisit only if they ask for it after using P1.

Does the project timeline drive mobilization dates automatically?

Recommend yes, as defaults only. Derive each item's due_date from the project start minus a lead time, and let admins override. Auto-derived-and-locked will be wrong constantly; entirely manual will simply never get filled in.

Roles in v1, when only admins use it?

Recommend model role and project_membership now, expose only admin. Retrofitting authorisation onto a system built assuming everyone sees everything is one of the more expensive refactors available, and PM access is clearly coming.

Multi-tenant?

Recommend no. One company. Do not pay the multi-tenancy tax for a hypothetical.

Audit log from day one?

Recommend yes, non-negotiable. This replaces a shared spreadsheet where everyone could see who changed what. Losing that visibility while asking people to trust a new system is how these rollouts die. ActivityEvent is one table and a write hook.

Where does the paint specification live?

Recommend structured fields, not an attachment (§3). Even in v1 where nothing computes from it, structure now is what lets consumable quantities derive from it later.

Currency handling?

Recommend store amount + currency, display natively, add a reporting rate only if they ask for a consolidated figure. No FX tables in v1.

Notifications in v1?

Recommend defer, with one exception worth considering: a daily digest email of at-risk and blocked items. It is cheap, and it drives people back into the tool, which is the main adoption risk.

Seed data strategy?

Recommend load two or three real past projects during P1, not synthetic data. Real projects will expose modelling gaps that invented ones politely hide.

§ 15 — Questions for the company

What to ask Parkk Marine

Seventy-eight questions, grouped so they can be split across several conversations. The bolded ones are the ones that would change the design if answered unexpectedly — ask those first.

A Business shape & the crux

Establishes scale and confirms we are building the right screen.

  1. If you could open one screen every morning and it answered one question, what would the question be? (We believe it is "what is not ready for the projects starting soonest" — we want to hear it in your words.)
  2. How many projects run concurrently today, and how many per year? What is the typical duration, and the longest and shortest you have run?
  3. How many workers on a typical project? On the largest you have done?
  4. Which countries and shipyards recur most? Are there yards you work with repeatedly whose rules we should store?
  5. What is the split between hull jobs and supervision jobs? Are there other kinds of work we have not accounted for — emergency call-outs, warranty work, dry-dock management, riding squads?
  6. Who exactly will use this in the first six months, by name and role, and on what device? Is anyone expected to use it from a shipyard?
  7. What lives in spreadsheets and Drive today that must move into this system, and what must deliberately stay outside it?

B Quotation & winning work

  1. Who prepares a quote, in what tool, and how long does it take?
  2. What actually goes into a quote — scope items, headcount by trade, day rates, equipment, materials, travel, margin?
  3. Do you want the estimate figures stored here for comparison against actuals, or is attaching the file enough?
  4. How many revision rounds are typical? Do you ever need to compare two versions side by side?
  5. What is the gap between "client accepted" and "confirmed"? Is there a PO, a contract, an LOI, or is a WhatsApp message enough?
  6. How firm are the dates in a quote? How often do start dates move after confirmation, and by how much?
  7. Do you quote in multiple currencies? Who decides the rate?
  8. Would you use win/loss reasons if we captured them — and what would the four or five options be?

C Hull job technical scope

The area we know least about, and the one that drives materials.

  1. Walk us through how a hull job is scoped: who inspects, what the initial condition report contains, and what form it arrives in.
  2. What does a paint specification consist of, and who issues it — you, the client, or the paint manufacturer?
  3. Which areas are typically quoted separately — flat bottom, vertical sides, boot-top, topsides, tanks, superstructure?
  4. Do you work to standard surface preparation grades, and do you need those recorded per area?
  5. Do you calculate paint quantities from area, coats and spreading rate? If we held the spec as structured data, would you want the consumables list derived from it?
  6. What inspection and QA happens during the job — DFT readings, holiday testing, client or manufacturer sign-off? Does any of it need tracking here?
  7. How is steel repair scoped and measured — by weight, by plate, by area — and does it get quoted separately from painting?
  8. For supervision jobs, what exactly are you supervising and what is the contractual deliverable?

D Confirmation & planning handover

  1. What is the typical lead time between confirmation and start date? What is the shortest you have ever had to work with?
  2. Is there a formal handover from whoever sold the job to the project manager? What gets handed over?
  3. Say out loud the checklist you run through in your head before a project starts. We want the version you actually use, not the tidy one.
  4. Which planning items strictly block others? (For instance, must a visa be approved before a yard pass can be applied for?)
  5. The owner column in your current tracker shows Abhi, Varsha and project managers. Is that split fixed, or does it vary by project?

E Manpower & sourcing

  1. Are workers your employees, agency-contracted, or a mix? Does the paperwork differ between them, and who is responsible for what in each case?
  2. Do you keep a master list of workers with their skills, or is a team assembled fresh each time?
  3. What trades should be in the standard list, and do you track certification levels within a trade — welder qualifications, blasting tickets, confined-space entry?
  4. How far in advance do you name specific people, versus planning with headcounts?
  5. Do people move directly from one project to the next? Would you want the system to warn you about double-booking someone?

F Immigration & work permits

  1. For your common nationality-to-country routes, what are the realistic lead times? These become the early-warning thresholds, so rough numbers per route are more useful to us than precision.
  2. Which routes are reliably painful, and which are effectively formalities?
  3. What stages would you want to see for a visa application? We have proposed: documents collected → filed → appointment → biometrics → approved → stamped → entered country. Is that right, and is one set of stages enough across all countries?
  4. Who files — you, the agency, an immigration lawyer, or the client? Do you need to record their reference numbers?
  5. Do work permits and visas ever expire mid-project? Do you handle extensions, and would you want them tracked?
  6. What happens when an application is refused? Is there a standard fallback, and how quickly can you substitute someone?
  7. Are there routes where people travel on business visas, or arrangements you would rather the system recorded discreetly?

G Travel & accommodation

  1. Who books flights — you, the client, or the agency? How often does that vary, and is it agreed per project or per client?
  2. You mentioned a travel request form. What is on it, who raises it, and who approves it?
  3. Do you need flight detail stored — routes, dates, PNR — or is "booked / not booked" plus a ticket attachment enough?
  4. Accommodation: hotels, company apartments, or yard-provided? Do you track room allocation and cost, or just that it is arranged?
  5. Is ground transport between hotel and yard something you arrange and would want tracked?
  6. How do you currently confirm someone has actually arrived on site, and who reports it?
  7. Are return flights booked up front or arranged at the end? How often do they change, and who absorbs the change fees?

H Shipyard access

  1. What does a yard typically demand before issuing passes — passport copies, photographs, safety training certificates, medical fitness, drug tests, insurance proof? How much does it vary between yards?
  2. How far ahead must the worker list be submitted, and how long does approval take?
  3. Are passes issued per person with fixed validity dates? What happens if a project overruns its passes?
  4. How are late crew changes and substitutions handled once a list has been submitted?
  5. Do yards or clients have their own submission format? If we could export it in their format, would that save real time?
  6. Are vehicle passes, tool passes or hot-work permits also needed, and are they per project or per person?

I Insurance & certification

  1. Which policies apply — travel, medical, workers' compensation, liability, P&I? Which are per worker and which per project?
  2. Who arranges each — you, the agency, or the client?
  3. You mentioned caveats. What kinds of exclusions matter in practice — geographic limits, hot-work exclusions, age limits — and would you want them visible per worker?
  4. Must coverage span the travel period, the working period, or both? Has a gap ever caused a problem?
  5. Which certifications expire and must stay valid for the whole project — medical fitness, safety training, trade tickets?

J Logistics, equipment & consumables

  1. How many warehouses, and where? Is there an existing system holding stock, or is it spreadsheets?
  2. Is there a standard materials list per job type that we could hold as a template, or is every job assembled from scratch?
  3. How do you decide between shipping owned equipment and renting locally? Who makes that call and when?
  4. Which shipment stages do you actually care about? We have proposed requested → packed → dispatched → in transit → customs → delivered to yard → received on site.
  5. Who handles freight and customs? Do you need their references and ETAs visible here?
  6. Does equipment carry certifications that expire and must be valid for the project duration?
  7. What happens to leftover consumables — returned, left on site, written off? Is consumption billable to the client?
  8. Partial deliveries: how common, and how do you currently know whether everything has arrived?

K Execution, reporting & change

  1. What is in a daily progress report, who writes it, and who reads it? Does the client get the same one you use internally?
  2. For supervision jobs, what is the reporting deliverable and its cadence, and what counts as it being done?
  3. How is scope creep handled commercially today — a formal variation order, a verbal agreement, or absorbed? And how often does it require extra people and new travel?
  4. What counts as a bottleneck worth logging? Who is meant to resolve them, and how are they escalated today?
  5. You track WhatsApp contact with the client. Would a simple "client updated today" flag be enough, or do you need the conversation itself somewhere?
  6. Are safety incidents and near-misses recorded anywhere, and should they be here?
  7. What formally ends a project — a client sign-off document, a final report, the last invoice?

L Commercial

  1. Do you want project cost tracked against the quoted value, or only status? If yes, which costs matter most — labour, travel, materials, equipment?
  2. How is invoicing structured — milestones, monthly, on completion? Is there retention or holdback?
  3. Who chases payment, and would seeing outstanding invoices on the project board be useful to them?

M Data, access & success

  1. Who is allowed to see passport and personal data, and how long may we keep documents after a project closes? Are there client contracts or national rules that constrain this?
  2. What other systems exist that this should link to or import from — accounting, HR, email, Drive? And, finally: what would make you say, a year from now, that this dashboard failed?
§ 16 — Assumptions & risks

What this design is standing on

Assumptions made in the absence of answers

  • Order of magnitude: tens of concurrent projects, tens of workers each, hundreds of trackable items per project. Nothing here needs to scale past that, and several choices would be different if it did.
  • Admins enter data manually; there are no integrations to pull status in automatically.
  • English throughout, one company, no white-labelling.
  • Documents are modest in number and size — scans and PDFs, not video.
  • The company is willing to keep a system current daily. If that is not true, no design choice rescues it.
